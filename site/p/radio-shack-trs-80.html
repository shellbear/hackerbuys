Oh man you gave me flashbacks. The TRS-80 Model II's OS, TRSDOS-II, had a built-in debugger that was little more than a monitor. You could step through instructions, examine and write to memory, set breakpoints to absolute memory locations, and that was it. I remember hand-assembling tiny Z80 programs in that thing and jumping into them, just to test my understanding of how machine code programs worked and how the computer executed them, and being super thrilled when I could get an A to appear somewhere on the screen or something. The machine had a much more complete assembly language programming toolkit which I also used to write more sophisticated programs, employing this debugger to examine them. But I felt like I'd "cracked the code" of the computer when I plugged hex numbers into RAM and then ran them straight from there. Most CPU ISA documentation should give you the opcodes that correspond to instruction mnemonics. You may have to plug in your own operands (registers, etc.) into bit fields in the instruction encoding. If you're serious about hand-assembling to begin with this should be no problem.

The Z80 will always be my first love, in a TRS-80 Model 1 and soon after, the Vector Graphic Vector 3 which was a sturdy S-100 machine running CP/M. Robert Harp of Vector was an extremely competent designer faced with a challenge, a computer with CPU, CRT and keyboard in one unit. All-in-one machines were a challenge because noise from the video stream and HV flyback transformer pervaded the insides, so he restricted the 6 card S-100 cage width to a few inches and found the worst transients on signal lines and placed extra conditioning on the bus, mods reported back to Zilog to assist with future Z80/S-100 builds. Thanks for the link! I had a TRS-80 Color Computer in the 1980s and had a lot of friends with Apple ][, C64 and even one with an Osborne 1. I was looking through old issues of Byte Magazine and was reminded that most of the computers of that age were pretty similar but that the TI-99/4A was rather different for a few reasons. One was that the CPU had no registers but instead the TI-99/4A had a small amount of fast RAM that was coupled directly to the CPU over a 16-bit bus. It had an 8-bit bus that interfaced to video RAM but you'd have to use I/O ports to write to it. It makes me think of things that people do where they put an Arduino on an S-100 bus (using GPIO to communicate) or FPGA-based systems where you might make the memory hierarchy explicit. (e.g. instead of having an SRAM cache, have an SRAM scratchpad and explicitly control transfers to DRAM.) Other home computers of that era had a simple frame buffer or something just a little more complex like what the Atari 400/800 had, if you were lucky you got a few sprites. The TI-99/4A had something more like the tile graphics on the Nintendo Entertainment System but people did some heroic things with it, for instance there was a Logo for the TI that could do turtle graphics by using tiles to emulate a frame buffer with an unusual order... with the detail that you could run out of tiles if you covered too much of the screen.
