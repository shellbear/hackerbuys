Likes it
I don't recall why I picked the FireGL versus the Quadro, I think the drivers looked better in 2001. I do recall we paid $2K for it which seemed like a lot at the time, but my personal annual budget for gfx cards is still $2K. Even before that, I had this insane software OpenGL called MetroX which was a full replacement for XFree86 that had native OpenGL support, allowing me to port a molecular graphics package (MidasPlus) to Linux (at the time, the authors used expensive SGIs and Alpha workstations). Later, Mesa replaced MetroX and was fast enough to do routine modelling for smaller systems, and eventually, with nvida T&L we could finally do decent large scale modelling (although the program used lines, which typically were not well-accelerated on the lower end cards that I could afford). At that point, the group I worked with looked at my machine (a linux box with a consumer grade GPU) and looked at their SGIs (including the abysmal SGI Visual Workstation running NT) and Alphas and saw the writing on the wall and eventually switched to Linux as their primary platform. It really was a perfect example of "first they laugh at you, then they fight you, then you win" for me.
dekhn, 2026-10-14 in “Nvidia's first GPU: GeForce 256”

I forget which early GPU-labelled card I got but when I got it, I aligned myself with nvidia (as a graphics provider) because it was clear that the pipelines could be re-used for other purposes (in my case, I wanted to run molecular dynamics simulations). The card replaced the previous one (ATI FireGL which had excellent OpenGL support for Linux early on) and I've stuck exclusively with nVidia since. They are the only company I've seen that saw the early vision and carried it through with focus through today. Nowadays I run a large fleet of nvidia GPUs for frontier research at work and also have the last few generations of cards in workstations at home.

Just SWAP is inflexible, especially with the way Linux usually handles buffer/pagecache, memory allocation,and countless other things, if not explicitly configured otherwise . You'd probably get the most out of it if you'd use https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zram setup to use that VRAM instead. Except if it has a very weak CPU which struggles with the load caused by compression, or power constraints, because mobile? But even then the compression is configurable. I once used something similar on an old Thinkpad 60p with an ATI FireGL which had some 32bit Intel Centrino, and had only 3.25GB usable RAM from the 4GB. The FireGL had 256MB of which I only needed 2, and let it have 6, just to be on the safe side. The other 250MB I gave to compcache pointed to the device created by some (experimental?) blockdriver I can't remember anymore. This wasn't automatic, didn't work on the first tries, and produced crashes. But was worth it once I had it right(¹), because that feeble thing ran much later into its limits. Noticably. Not by some meaningless benchmarks. (¹) much fiddling with many hexadecimal memory addresses and ranges, exclusions of the same in other configuration files, scattered over /etc/, mostly Xorg, but also other Drivers/PCI-Address spaces, to not have one BYTE of overlap , but also not wasting any ;-) Edit: Skimming the two links phram rings a bell.
