My experience with Linux (dabbling frequently over the past decade and some change) has been extremely variable. When it’s solid, it’s extremely solid, but more frequently there were weird quirks of some type or another. The best experiences I’ve had are on Intel machines lacking dedicated graphics (iGPU only) with Intel networking/Bluetooth. Any variance from that increase chances of oddities, especially if your machine has the misfortune of being built with Broadcom hardware — though it’s not too difficult to get most Broadcom wifi/Bluetooth working, it’s likely to stop working after random updates. Nvidia GPUs with multiple screens can act weird (at least under X11), especially if you have more than one GPU. AMD GPUs are better behaved but still not as smooth as under Windows or even macOS. Laptops come with their own quirks too, like one of mine has volume buttons that might or might not work depending on which DE you’re running or if you’ve got the right daemon running in your minimal WM setup. And of course the DEs themselves have bits of weirdness. The most functional with fewest oddities I’ve run is XFCE, which might be boring by the standards of some, but it feels like the team behind it has its priorities more in order than those of either GNOME or KDE. Cinnamon is a strong contender but from what I can see lacks resources. Pantheon would be nice but has a few (to me) fatal design flaws. I could probably replace Windows with Linux if I really wanted to because I can…

On the PC we have only three GPU vendors: AMD, NVIDIA and Intel, all three of which have been around for decades and have substantial commercial contracts to deliver stuff that at least isn't horribly broken, as well as the users regularly updating their drivers in case bugs (or, for major AAA titles, hand-optimized shaders) appear. Meanwhile in embedded space, Broadcom can't even be arsed to implement PCI Express according to spec for a product of the scale and importance of the Raspberry Pi[1], and Android users have literally no say in updating their devices because they don't even have root access on them. Assuming someone discovers a bug in the GPU side, the bug has to go from the discoverer to the device manufacturer, from there to the SoC vendor, hope that the SoC vendor is willing and able to fix the bug, then that has to go back to the manufacturer, who has to prepare a firmware update, test and certify that with phone carriers and their bloatware, and then ship the updated firmware. As you can guess from the process, that rarely happens for anything except the flagship devices from Samsung and Google. Anything outside of the x86, mainframe and Apple world can plainly be summarized as "it's a horrible horrible mess". [1] https://www.tomshardware.com/news/raspberry-pi-gpu-success
