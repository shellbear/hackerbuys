Neutral
In Fall of 1984, a friend bought one of these for his Commodore 64. He talked me into buying one for my C64. We chatted clumsily with the spartan terminal software for a while. I had an ad for a local BBS in a magazine. We called that BBS which had a list of numbers for other BBS's in town. We started calling those. During those days of calling a lot of BBS's on a regular basis, I was calling on a rotary-dial phone. That got to be hard on the finger if you encountered a busy signal. It wasn't too long before my friend and I each picked up a Westridge sutomodem ... which was a workalike for the Commodore 1650 automodem ( both the Westridge and the 1650 had auto-answer and auto-dial (pulse) capabilities. If I remember correctly, on a C64, you could push the vicmodem to 400 and 450 baud for boards that supported it, but the communications errors were higher. It garbled characters frequently ... usually attributed to line-noise. In the latter 80's, I had gotten rid of my old computer gear and was getting ready to buy something new. In the interim, I picked up a VIC-20 with a vicmodem for about $25. That allowed me to visit the local BBS's until I got some better hardware.

I alarmed my teachers by skipping recess to use the computer and read every issue of Creative Computing and Byte I could get my hands on. I begged my parents to get me an Apple for at least a year before they relented - partially - and bought me the VIC a few months after it came out. A twentieth of the RAM of a fully-loaded Apple and a 22-column display? No problem, you could still do a lot with that! Less than a year later, I persuaded them to buy me the newly-released VICmodem. First it was a scientific calculator, then a programmable scientific calculator. Then he got one of those devices originally made by Sharp but badge-engineered by Radio Shack which they called a "pocket computer"; very calculator-like, but had an alphanumeric keyboard and could store and run BASIC programs written on it with a one-line display. Ultimately he bit the bullet and went back to Radio Shack to buy the most powerful computer they sold at the time: the TRS-80 Model 16. It was a variant of their TRS-80 Model II business-class computer but with a 68000 daughtercard with a quarter to half a meg of memory. He used this to finally write the engine simulations he'd been planning in his head all along, replete with graphical curve plots and an animation of the piston turning the crankshaft. It ran at about four seconds per frame, but it was still spectacular to behold. I was fascinated. I sat there and watched him type in the programs line by line.
